# Concept and judgment

## Establish what recognition has to accomplish

An app icon helps someone find a product repeatedly. A toolbar symbol helps someone infer an action now. A document icon identifies an artifact and its family. These jobs tolerate different amounts of ornament, ambiguity, and variation. An elaborate product illustration does not become a good 18-point toolbar symbol by being shrunk.

Write a short brief before drawing:

- Human purpose: “find and compare my field observations,” rather than a list of implementation features.
- Role and surface: Dock app, iPhone Home Screen app, Finder file type, menu-bar item, toolbar action.
- Recognition anchor: the shape or relationship that survives monochrome, reduction, and recoloring.
- Personality: two specific properties, such as precise and tactile. Translate each into choices of geometry, material, spacing, or color.
- Constraints: existing brand, forbidden confusion, required family resemblance, deliverable and target versions.
- Acceptance: what someone must recognize and at which viewing sizes.

A metaphor need not teach the entire product. A distinctive learned identity may be stronger than a literal feature collage. Familiar objects offer an immediate foothold, but their associations vary with culture and experience. Explain the intended association and test confusion with nearby categories. Avoid assuming that every person understands a floppy disk, drafting compass, or obsolete device.

## Generate genuinely different candidates

Use associations in three directions: the user's object, the user's action, and the outcome they want. Pick two or three concepts with different silhouettes or object relationships. Render rough black shapes and one accent. For a field-observation app, a specimen window, a marked trail, and a lens enclosing terrain are three concepts; three different green lenses are one concept.

Each candidate gets the same canvas, viewing size, and level of finish. A polished weak idea can unfairly beat a rough strong idea. Reject concepts that require captions, multiple tiny props, or background detail to be distinguishable. Keep the discarded roughs and short reasons; later changes should not unknowingly restore a rejected lane.

Do not multiply candidates forever. Once a brief and a readable direction exist, build a proof at both small and large scale. The small proof determines whether the premise works; the large proof determines whether its personality can be developed.

## Judge structure before surface

Inspect in this order:

1. **Silhouette:** distinct and coherent? Are projecting parts deliberate? Do objects merge accidentally?
2. **Mass and negative space:** is the foreground balanced within the canvas and among neighbors? Are narrow gaps still open at intended size?
3. **Value hierarchy:** does the hero separate from its support without depending only on hue?
4. **Form:** do perspective, overlaps, and highlights describe one plausible object arrangement?
5. **Material:** do metal, paper, paint, and glass differ through characteristic responses?
6. **Detail:** does texture support a larger structure, or become independent noise?
7. **Context:** does the icon retain character without dominating every adjacent icon?

A grayscale check is a diagnostic for value separation. It does not prove color-vision accessibility or automatically make a tint variant. Alpha centroid, occupied area, and bounding box can expose misalignment, but a visually heavy dark corner is not measured by alpha alone. Compare human-visible balance directly.

## What aesthetic quality looks like operationally

A strong icon often contains a deliberate hierarchy of frequencies: a memorable large shape, a few medium-scale forms that explain it, and fine detail only where the first two already work. “More detail” is not equivalent to more information. A small bright reflection that explains a curved handle can be valuable; fifty unrelated speckles may only make the object dirty.

Restraint does not require flatness. A richly rendered icon can remain simple in its silhouette and grouping. Conversely, a flat icon can be busy, generic, or badly balanced. Choose flat, dimensional, playful, archival, or severe treatment because it serves the identity and delivery context, not because the renderer makes a particular effect easy.

Idealization is part of illustration. Real paper is imperfect, but exaggerating a curl can clarify its material. Real metal reflects its whole environment, but a small icon may need only a bright band next to a dark band. Preserve the causal cue; simplify the incidental complexity. Exaggeration should improve recognition rather than turn every edge into a bevel.

Generic polish has recurring symptoms: a stock squircle with an unrelated white symbol; repeated purple-blue gradients without a brand reason; every object glossy; bevels used to disguise uncertain contours; glows replacing contrast; three metaphors competing for the same visual weight. Fix the idea or structure before increasing effects.

## Critique format for an agent

Name the visible defect, the context where it happens, its likely cause, and the smallest useful change. For example: “At 16 points the handle joins the ring into a blob; their shared dark boundary disappears. Increase the gap by half a logical point and remove the rim highlight at this size.” Avoid vague prescriptions like “make it premium.”

Separate observation from taste: “The two silhouettes become identical at 16 points” is inspectable. “The gold feels too ceremonial for this utility” is a reasoned preference tied to the brief. Give the user the latter as a judgment, not an objective failure.
